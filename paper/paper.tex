\title{\textbf{Predicting the Discrete-Log Learnability Boundary:}\\
Representation, Spectral Accessibility, and Grokking}

\author{David Vesterlund}

\date{September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We investigate whether representation-level and architecture-conditioned spectral measurements obtained \emph{before training} can prospectively predict whether a discrete-logarithm (DLP) formulation will directly generalize, grok after memorization, memorize without generalizing within a fixed compute horizon, or fail to fit. Our study tests two predictor classes: (A) a strict pre-training predictor using only $t=0$ quantities, and (B) an early-training predictor using a pre-registered 1\% probe window. The primary causal representation intervention uses information-preserving GF(2) affine bijections on fixed-length binary encodings: $r_{A,c}(z) = Az + c \bmod 2$ where $A \in \text{GL}(B, 2)$ and $c \in \{0,1\}^B$. These transforms preserve bit length, alphabet, information content, and task labels while changing only the coordinate geometry. We position our work relative to Kumar et al.\ (lazy-to-rich transition), Mohamadi et al.\ (kernel-regime limitations), Kam et al.\ (algebraic representability), and Song \& Ye (competing memorization/generalization timescales). The central evidentiary chain is: \emph{define predictor $\to$ freeze predictor $\to$ freeze configurations $\to$ predict unseen runs $\to$ commit predictions $\to$ train $\to$ evaluate without refitting}. If the spectral predictor does not outperform simple scale/data baselines, we report that result rather than changing the metric until it does.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The \emph{grokking} phenomenon \citep{power2022grokking}---in which a neural network abruptly transitions from memorization to generalization long after overfitting---has been studied extensively on modular arithmetic tasks. A natural question is whether grokking extends to the discrete logarithm problem (DLP), a structured inverse problem on cyclic groups.

A prior study \citep{vesterlund2026grokking} demonstrated that Transformers can grok the variable-base finite-field DLP at $p=113$, including in a prime-order subgroup of order $q=113$. This result coexists with the theoretical hardness result of \citet{takhanov2024intractability}, who proved that gradient-based methods cannot efficiently learn the parity bit of the discrete logarithm in cyclic groups of large prime order.

The missing object is not merely the \emph{learnability boundary}---the surface separating successful generalization from failure---but the ability to \emph{predict} it before training. This paper asks:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can representation-level and architecture-conditioned spectral measurements obtained before training predict whether a DLP formulation will directly generalize, grok after memorization, memorize without generalizing, or fail to fit?}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Literature positioning.}
We explicitly position relative to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \citet{kumar2023grokking}: Grokking as the transition from lazy to rich training dynamics. Initial feature/NTK alignment and lazy-to-rich feature learning already exist as explanations of grokking. We do NOT claim NTK-target alignment itself is novel.
    \item \citet{mohamadi2024grok}: Kernel-regime predictors can fail on modular addition because later feature learning escapes the initial kernel regime. A static $t=0$ measure may fail despite later grokking.
    \item \citet{kam2026algebraic}: Algebraic representability as the limiting regime of grokking. Representability is a prior gate.
    \item \citet{song2025capacity}: Model capacity determines grokking through competing memorization and generalization speeds. $T_{\text{mem}}$ and $T_{\text{gen}}$ are distinct measurable timescales.
    \item \citet{takhanov2024intractability}: Gradient-based hardness of learning the discrete-log parity concept.
    \item \citet{nanda2023progress}: Fourier/mechanistic structure in grokking modular addition.
\end{itemize}

The novelty we are testing is NOT ``NTK alignment matters'' and NOT ``representation matters.'' The intended novelty is: \emph{whether spectral and gradient accessibility can prospectively predict learning phase and time-to-generalization across DLP formulations and information-preserving representations.}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item We design a prospective prediction protocol with strict locking: predictions must precede the training they predict. Two predictor classes (pre-training and early-probe) are tested separately.
    \item We use information-preserving GF(2) affine bijections as the primary causal representation intervention: same bits, same length, same alphabet, same information, same labels, different coordinate geometry.
    \item We define spectral accessibility features (Fourier entropy, kernel spectral accessibility $T_{\text{spec}}$, KSA$_\tau$) and gradient accessibility features measured at initialization.
    \item We pre-register hypotheses and success criteria before confirmatory experiments.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Grokking and Training Dynamics}

\citet{power2022grokking} introduced grokking on modular arithmetic. \citet{kumar2023grokking} explained grokking as a transition from lazy to rich training dynamics, showing that initial feature/NTK alignment predicts which tasks will grok. \citet{mohamadi2024grok} showed that kernel-regime predictors can fail on modular addition because later feature learning escapes the initial kernel regime, meaning a static $t=0$ measure may fail despite later grokking.

\subsection{Algebraic Representability and Capacity}

\citet{kam2026algebraic} established algebraic representability as the limiting regime of grokking. \citet{song2025capacity} showed that model capacity determines grokking through competing memorization and generalization speeds, with $T_{\text{mem}}$ and $T_{\text{gen}}$ as distinct measurable timescales.

\subsection{DLP Hardness}

\citet{takhanov2024intractability} proved that gradient-based methods require exponentially many steps (in $\log q$) to learn the parity bit of the discrete logarithm in cyclic groups of prime order. The proof relies on gradient concentration: the expected gradient becomes exponentially small as $q$ grows.

\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability}

\citet{nanda2023progress} analyzed Fourier/mechanistic structure in grokking modular addition, showing that networks learn Fourier features.

\section{DLP Learning Tasks}\label{sec:tasks}

\subsection{Cyclic Groups}

For a prime $q$, we consider two realizations of the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_q$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Additive: $G_{\text{add}} = (\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}, +)$
    \item Multiplicative: $G_{\text{mult}} = \langle g_0 \rangle \subset \mathbb{F}_p^*$ with $|G_{\text{mult}}| = q$, where $p = 2q+1$ is a safe prime.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Task Types}

\paragraph{Variable-base full-log DLP.} Given base $g$ and target $h = g^x$, predict $x$.

\paragraph{Fixed-base parity concept.} Given fixed base $g$ and target $h = g^x$, predict $\text{bit}_i(x)$. This corresponds to the concept class in \citet{takhanov2024intractability}.

\section{Representations and Information-Preserving Encodings}\label{sec:encodings}

\subsection{Primary Intervention: GF(2) Affine Bijections}

The main causal representation experiment uses information-preserving affine bijections on fixed-length binary representations:
\begin{equation}
r_{A,c}(z) = Az + c \bmod 2
\end{equation}
where $A \in \text{GL}(B, 2)$ (invertible $B \times B$ matrix over GF(2)) and $c \in \{0,1\}^B$.

This transform preserves:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Bit length (sequence length unchanged)
    \item Alphabet (binary $\{0,1\}$)
    \item Information content (bijection)
    \item Task labels (labels depend on latent $x$, not on encoding)
    \item Latent examples (same manifest)
\end{itemize}

Only the coordinate geometry changes. This is a cleaner causal intervention than comparing additive vs.\ multiplicative groups, which changes too many things simultaneously.

\subsection{Secondary: Additive vs.\ Multiplicative}

Retained as a motivating secondary demonstration. Same latent $(a, x)$ pairs, different group realizations. Not the primary causal test.

\subsection{Encoding Families}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Identity binary: $r(z) = z$
    \item Gray code: standard binary-to-Gray mapping
    \item GF(2) affine: $r(z) = Az + c \bmod 2$ (primary intervention)
    \item Feistel: keyed nonlinear bijection (for OOD holdout)
    \item Atomic integer: one token per residue (secondary, vocabulary differs)
\end{itemize}

\section{Spectral Accessibility}\label{sec:spectral}

\subsection{Fourier Features}

For the target function on a cyclic group of order $q$, we compute:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Fourier entropy $H_F$
    \item Effective support $\exp(H_F)$
    \item Participation ratio $\text{PR}_F$
    \item Top-$k$ energy concentration ($k \in \{1, 4, 8, 16\}$)
    \item Spectral effective rank
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kernel Spectral Accessibility}

Let $K = U \Lambda U^T$ be the kernel eigendecomposition and $C_j = U_j^T Y$ the target projection onto kernel eigenvectors. Under fixed-kernel gradient flow, the normalized residual energy is:
\begin{equation}
E(s) = \frac{\sum_j \|C_j\|^2 \exp(-2\lambda_j s)}{\sum_j \|C_j\|^2}
\end{equation}

The \emph{spectral accessibility time} is:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{spec}}(\epsilon) = \inf\{s : E(s) \leq \epsilon\}
\end{equation}
with primary $\epsilon = 0.10$.

The \emph{Kernel Spectral Accessibility} score is:
\begin{equation}
\text{KSA}_\tau = \frac{\sum_j \frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_j + \tau} \|C_j\|^2}{\|Y\|^2}
\end{equation}
with primary scale $\tau_0 = \text{tr}(K)/n$.

\section{Fixed-Kernel Theory}\label{sec:theory}

For squared loss under linearized gradient flow:
\begin{equation}
r(t) = \exp(-Kt) \, r(0)
\end{equation}
and therefore spectral residual energy evolves according to kernel eigenvalues. We derive a proposition connecting target spectral mass, kernel eigenvalues, and $\epsilon$ hitting time to $T_{\text{spec}}$.

We clearly state:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Exact assumptions (squared loss, linearized dynamics)
    \item Whether results apply to gradient flow (yes) vs.\ Adam/AdamW (approximate)
    \item Why empirical validation is still required
\end{itemize}

\section{Prospective Prediction Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Two Predictor Classes}

\paragraph{P0: Strict Pre-Training.} Uses only $t=0$ quantities: $q$, $N$, $P$, target type, optimizer config, representation descriptors, Fourier descriptors, kernel alignment, $T_{\text{spec}}$, KSA$_\tau$, initialization gradient features. May NOT use any training results.

\paragraph{P1: Early-Probe.} Runs exactly 1\% of training budget, then pauses. Computes kernel/gradient drift features. Makes and locks new predictions. Then resumes remaining 99\%.

\subsection{Locking Protocol}

No confirmatory run may start without a valid prediction lock. Locks are append-only and contain SHA256 hashes of all relevant files. The launcher refuses to start confirmatory jobs unless \texttt{verify\_lock} passes.

\subsection{No Peeking Rule}

During confirmatory phases:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Do not inspect test curves before predictions are locked
    \item Do not change $\tau$, $\epsilon$, feature sets, phase thresholds, or model coefficients
    \item Do not change selected encodings
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Study Layers}

Every run belongs to exactly one evidence class:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \texttt{LEGACY\_EXPLORATORY}: pre-protocol runs, calibration only
    \item \texttt{CALIBRATION}: used to develop and fit the predictor
    \item \texttt{CONFIRMATORY}: used for blind prospective evaluation
\end{itemize}

\section{Calibration Experiments}\label{sec:calibration}

\subsection{CAL\_A: Clean q=113 Pilot}

40 runs: 4 encodings (identity, Gray, GF2 seed A, GF2 seed B) $\times$ 2 optimization regimes $\times$ 5 seeds. Evidence class: CALIBRATION.

\subsection{CAL\_B: Boundary Discovery Grid}

216 runs: 3 groups $\times$ 2 tasks $\times$ 4 encodings $\times$ 3 train fractions $\times$ 3 seeds. Purpose: discover phase transitions, train initial predictor.

\subsection{CAL\_C: Capacity Sweep}

120 runs: 6 boundary configurations $\times$ 4 capacities $\times$ 5 seeds. Estimate $T_{\text{mem}}(P)$ and $T_{\text{gen}}(P)$.

\subsection{CAL\_D: Optimization Sweep}

120 runs: 6 configurations $\times$ 4 optimization conditions $\times$ 5 seeds.

\subsection{CAL\_R: Random-Label Controls}

72 runs: 6 configurations $\times$ 4 capacities $\times$ 3 seeds. Estimate memorization timescale separately from algorithmic generalization.

\section{Blind Confirmatory Experiments}\label{sec:confirmatory}

\subsection{CONF\_A: Prospective GF(2) Encoding Prediction}

270 runs: 3 held-out group orders $\times$ 18 encodings (6 high + 6 mid + 6 low predicted accessibility) $\times$ 5 seeds. P0 lock before training, P1 lock at 1\%.

\subsection{CONF\_B: Design/Steering Experiment}

100 runs: 4th held-out $q$, search GF($B$,2) space for high/low accessibility encodings. Tests whether predictor can construct representations that systematically shift $T_{\text{gen}}$.

\subsection{CONF\_C: Out-of-Family Encoding Holdout}

120 runs: 2 held-out $q$ values $\times$ 6 encodings (identity + 5 Feistel) $\times$ 2 train fractions $\times$ 5 seeds. Tests transfer beyond linear GF(2).

\subsection{CONF\_D: Isomorphic Group Demonstration}

20 runs: 2 group sizes $\times$ 2 realizations (additive/multiplicative) $\times$ 5 seeds. Secondary illustration.

\section{Outcome Definitions}\label{sec:outcomes}

Using sustained thresholds ($K=10$ consecutive evaluations):
\begin{align}
T_{\text{mem}} &= \min\{t : A_{\text{train}}(t) \geq 0.99 \text{ for } K \text{ evals}\} \\
T_{\text{gen}} &= \min\{t : A_{\text{test}}(t) \geq 0.90 \text{ for } K \text{ evals}\}
\end{align}

Phase taxonomy:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
    \item[Direct generalization:] $T_{\text{gen}} \leq T_{\text{mem}}$
    \item[Grokking:] $T_{\text{mem}} < T_{\text{gen}} \leq T_{\text{budget}}$
    \item[Memorized (censored):] $T_{\text{mem}}$ exists, $T_{\text{gen}}$ not observed before $T_{\text{budget}}$
    \item[Underfit:] $T_{\text{mem}}$ not observed before $T_{\text{budget}}$
    \item[Partial:] intermediate sustained generalization $< 0.90$
\end{description}

\section{Statistical Analysis}\label{sec:statistics}

\subsection{Primary Endpoint}

Prospective prediction of censored $T_{\text{gen}}$ using survival/time-to-event metrics. Primary comparison: FULL P0 vs.\ Baseline 2. Success criterion: $\Delta$ concordance-index $\geq 0.05$ with 95\% cluster-bootstrap CI excluding zero.

\subsection{Grouped Cross-Validation}

Configuration is the statistical unit. GroupKFold by $q$ and encoding family. Seeds from one configuration remain in the same fold.

\section{Pre-Registered Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
    \item[H1:] Pre-training spectral accessibility predicts time-to-generalization after controlling for $q$, $N$, model size, target type, and optimization.
    \item[H2:] Information-preserving GF(2) bijections of the same DLP input cause systematic differences in learning dynamics, partly explained by spectral accessibility.
    \item[H3:] On unseen group orders, encodings ranked high-accessibility before training generalize faster than low-accessibility.
    \item[H4:] A locked predictor can construct high-accessibility encodings that systematically improve $T_{\text{gen}}$.
    \item[H5:] If $t=0$ kernel information is insufficient, early lazy-to-rich movement during the 1\% probe provides additional prospective predictive information.
\end{description}

\section{Success Decision Tree}\label{sec:decision}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
    \item[Case A:] P0 beats Baseline 2 $\Rightarrow$ ``Pre-training spectral accessibility prospectively predicts DLP learnability dynamics.''
    \item[Case B:] P0 fails, P1 succeeds $\Rightarrow$ ``Early kernel/feature dynamics prospectively predict DLP learnability dynamics.'' (Not called pre-training prediction.)
    \item[Case C:] Neither improves over baselines $\Rightarrow$ Report negative result: ``Initial kernel accessibility does not contain sufficient information to predict rich-regime DLP generalization.''
\end{description}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Our experiments use small primes ($q \leq 2^{18}$). The hardness theorem is asymptotic.
    \item The predictor is calibrated on a limited set of groups and encodings.
    \item GF(2) affine bijections are linear; nonlinear encodings (Feistel) may behave differently.
    \item The kernel spectral accessibility is a fixed-kernel proxy; Adam/AdamW dynamics differ.
    \item No experiment at small $q$ constitutes a practical DLP attack.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This study is designed to produce an interesting paper under multiple outcomes. If the $t=0$ predictor works, we have a strong result: the representation's initial spectral geometry contains information about future grokking. If $t=0$ fails but the 1\% probe works, we have empirical evidence that the lazy-to-rich transition, rather than initial kernel alignment, is what becomes predictive. If both fail, we have a substantial negative result: the generalizing DLP solution is so much a product of later feature learning that neither initial kernel geometry nor very early changes suffice to predict it.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have designed a prospective prediction study with strict locking protocols to determine whether spectral accessibility can predict DLP learnability. The primary causal intervention uses GF(2) affine bijections---information-preserving transforms that change only coordinate geometry. Results from confirmatory experiments will determine which (if any) of the pre-registered hypotheses are supported.
